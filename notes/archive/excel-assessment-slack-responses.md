### 8. Joni Factor, 16:46 (how scorers weigh the dimensions)

> @Toby Young @Gina Muan - To the question of how do scorers weigh excel skills.
>
> Based on the 240 evaluations in Form Responses 1, reviewers are not just grading a spreadsheet test—they are evaluating whether a candidate can operate as a client-facing analyst (frequently referred to in the comments as an MRA or CAI analyst).
>
> While the six questions test technical tasks (filtering, charting, aggregating, and comparing), the comments and final Yes/No recommendations show that reviewers evaluate five core areas—with a very clear hierarchy in how they weigh them.
>
> **1. What Is Actually Being Evaluated?**
>
> Beyond getting the right numbers, scorers are looking for:
>
> - **Metric Literacy:** Whether the candidate truly understands what win share (carrier wins / total wins across all carriers) and percent change ((new - old) / old) mean in practice.
> - **Workflow Transparency:** Whether the reviewer can trace how the candidate got their numbers (using Pivot Tables or clear formulas in the Workbook tabs) rather than seeing hardcoded values.
> - **Client-Ready Polish:** Whether charts, tables, and written explanations are clean, properly formatted, and free of careless errors.
> - **Analytical Storytelling (The "So What?"):** Whether the candidate can go beyond describing a chart to explain why trends are happening (e.g., promotions, seasonality, network coverage, competitor dynamics) and propose relevant next steps.
> - **Authenticity (2026 trend):** In recent evaluations, reviewers actively screen for suspected AI usage (e.g., overly engineered SUMIFS/IFERROR chains instead of Pivot Tables, italicized footnotes, or generic/hyper-specific LLM phrasing), which has become a standalone reason for rejection even when every number is correct.
>
> **2. How Scorers Weigh the Four Dimensions**
>
> **1. Conceptual Understanding — The Hardest Gatekeeper (Highest Weight)**
>
> Conceptual understanding is treated as non-negotiable. If a candidate fundamentally misunderstands what win share represents, they are almost universally rejected (No), even if their Excel formatting is clean or their written answers are thoughtful.
>
> - **Common dealbreakers:** Graphing win volume (raw wins) instead of win share; calculating win distribution (market X Sprint wins / total Sprint wins); dividing wins by population; or summing percentages across months/markets (producing >100% win shares).
> - **Percent Change vs. Absolute Change:** Confusing (new - old) / old with percentage-point difference (new - old) is heavily penalized. Some reviewers treat it as an automatic No, while others will forgive it only if the rest of the assessment is exceptionally strong.
> - **Key takeaway:** Conceptual errors trump Excel mastery. Reviewers explicitly reject candidates who show strong formula skills but calculate the wrong metric, while often passing candidates who make minor Excel mistakes if their conceptual logic is sound ("trainable").
>
> **2. Instruction Following — Very High Weight (Cascading Impact)**
>
> Because the assessment is sequential, failing to follow instructions early on creates a snowball effect—and reviewers treat repeated misses as a major red flag.
>
> - **The Question 1 "Sprint-Only" Trap:** A large share of candidates filter the Winner column to Sprint Wireless only (leaving ~541 rows instead of 2,657) or miss the Northeast + Midwest and population > 500,000 filters.
> - **How it's weighed:** If a candidate makes this mistake in Q1 but recognizes it and pulls all carriers for Q2–Q6, most reviewers only dock Q1 and still pass them. If they let the bad filter cascade through every question without noticing, it usually results in a No.
> - **Prompt Precision:** Reviewers frequently dock points—and reject borderline candidates—for ignoring explicit instructions such as:
>     - Using CMAs vs. Subregions (or CMA IDs instead of names).
>     - Following the percent-change definition in the Resources tab.
>     - Pasting final answers and charts into the Answers tab rather than leaving them in Workbook tabs.
>     - Providing multiple comparisons in Question 6 (which asks for "comparisons" plural).
>
> **3. Attention to Detail — Medium-High Weight (The Deciding Tiebreaker)**
>
> Because the role involves client-facing deliverables, scorers treat sloppiness as a disqualifier—even when the math is 100% correct.
>
> - **Sanity Checking:** Reviewers expect candidates to catch obvious red flags, such as rounding errors causing mismatched numbers between Q3 and Q4 (both use August 2014 data), or impossible outputs like 0% or 400% win share.
> - **Formatting & Hygiene:** Leaving win shares as 10-digit unformatted decimals, leaving chart axes as raw numbers or Dec-99 dates, failing to sort top-5 tables, or copying/pasting mismatched rows.
> - **Written Polish:** Multiple reviewers explicitly give a No to candidates with 100% correct calculations due to repeated typos, incomplete/run-on sentences, or copy-pasting the exact same short-answer text across Q3b, Q4b, and Q5a.
>
> **4. Excel Skills — Medium Weight (Valued for Clarity, Not Complexity)**
>
> Excel proficiency is treated as foundational and trainable, rather than a differentiator on its own.
>
> - **Simplicity & Transparency > Advanced Formulas:** Reviewers strongly prefer Pivot Tables paired with straightforward lookups (VLOOKUP/XLOOKUP). Candidates who use helper columns, manual filtering, or SUMIFS are rarely rejected as long as their work is visible, logical, and accurate.
> - **Hardcoding is a Red Flag:** Pasting values without formulas or Pivot Tables makes it impossible for reviewers to verify the candidate's process and consistently hurts their score.
> - **Over-engineering Backfires:** Using overly complex nested SUMIFS + IFERROR formulas instead of Pivot Tables often triggers suspicion of AI assistance rather than impressing scorers.
>
> **Summary Ranking (From Most to Least Decisive)**
>
> 1. **Conceptual Understanding:** Must-have. Getting the definition of win share wrong almost always results in a No.
> 2. **Instruction Following:** High impact. One isolated miss is forgiven; repeated misses (wrong geography, wrong tab, missing visuals, ignoring definitions) lead to rejection.
> 3. **Attention to Detail (+ Analytical Storytelling):** The differentiator. Separates a "soft yes / maybe" from a "strong yes," and frequently turns a mathematically perfect assessment into a No if the writing is sloppy or lacks insight.
> 4. **Excel Skills:** Baseline requirement. Reviewers care that you can filter, join, pivot, and chart cleanly without hardcoding—advanced formula gymnastics are unnecessary and can even raise AI flags.
